# Understanding South African Crime Statistics

Crime numbers are easy to misread. This guide explains where the data comes from and how to read it responsibly.

## Where the data comes from

| Source | What it covers | Where to find it |
|---|---|---|
| **SAPS crime statistics** | Crimes *reported* to police, released quarterly, broken down by province, district and police precinct | saps.gov.za |
| **Stats SA Victims of Crime Survey (VOCS)** | A household survey that asks people about crime they experienced, whether or not they reported it | statssa.gov.za |
| **Academic and NGO research** | Deeper analysis, for example from the Institute for Security Studies (ISS) | issafrica.org |

## Key ideas

### 1. Reported crime is not all crime
SAPS statistics only count crime that was **reported**. Many crimes, especially gender-based violence, fraud and theft, go unreported. The Victims of Crime Survey helps fill the gap.

### 2. Use rates, not just totals
A big city will have more crimes than a small town simply because more people live there. Compare **rates per 100,000 people**:

```
rate = (number of incidents / population) x 100,000
```

### 3. More reports does not always mean more crime
If trust in police improves or a campaign encourages reporting, numbers can rise even when actual crime stays the same.

### 4. Precinct boundaries are not neighbourhoods
Data is published per police precinct. A precinct can cover very different areas, so do not use it to label a suburb or community.

### 5. Look at trends, not single quarters
Crime fluctuates. Compare the same quarter across several years to spot real changes.

## Responsible use checklist

- [ ] Cite the source and the date range
- [ ] Say whether figures are totals or per 100,000
- [ ] Do not stigmatise an area, race or group based on numbers
- [ ] Never combine statistics with personal information
- [ ] Note any limits (for example, "reported crime only")
